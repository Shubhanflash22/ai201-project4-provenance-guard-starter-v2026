# Acceptance criteria — Provenance Guard

Five criteria that say what "working" means for this service, written in unit 7
**before** you built it.

**All five are yours.** None are given.

An acceptance criterion names a number or something plainly observable. *"The
detector is accurate"* is an opinion — and perfect AI detection isn't possible
anyway, so it's an opinion about something nobody can deliver. *"Of 10 pieces I
wrote myself, at most 1 is labelled high-confidence AI"* is a criterion.

Under each, write a sentence or two on **why that target**. *"I allow at most 1
false positive in 10 because on a writing site a wrong accusation costs more
than a missed detection"* is a real answer.

> Missing your own targets next unit costs you nothing. Setting a target so
> easy you can't miss it does.

---

## Pick numbers you can defend

**Cover at least three of these five areas.** They're prompts, not a form.

| Area | A question it could answer |
|---|---|
| False positives | How often may a human's writing get called AI? |
| Score spread | How far apart should clearly-AI and clearly-human text score? |
| Label coverage | Is every one of your three labels actually reachable? |
| The appeal path | Does an appeal always change status and always get recorded? |
| Bad input | What happens when `text` is missing, empty, or enormous? |

Two things worth knowing before you pick numbers:

- **The expensive mistake is one-sided.** Missing some AI text costs the site a
  little. Accusing a real writer costs that person a lot. A criterion that
  treats both errors as equally bad is describing a system nobody would ship.
- **Unit 8 tests across three trials, and the target has to hold across all
  three.** Your first signal runs a model, so identical input can score
  differently twice. A target of "at most 1 in 10" against trials of 2, 1, 2 is
  a **miss**.

## 1. False positives: my own writing is rarely called AI

Of 10 pieces I wrote myself, at most 1 receives the label "high-confidence AI" (guess `ai`). Labels "unsure" and "high-confidence human" don't count against it. This must hold in all three trials.

**Why this target:** On a writing site, wrongly accusing a real writer costs that person far more than missing some AI text costs the site, so I allow at most 1 in 10. I didn't pick 0 because no detector built on gpt2 and sentence statistics can promise that, and I didn't pick 3 because a rate that high would mean accusing real writers routinely.

---

## 2. Score spread: clearly-AI and clearly-human text land apart

I submit 5 pairs of texts. In each pair, one is clearly AI (generated by an AI tool, unedited) and one is clearly human (written by me, in my normal style). In at least 4 of the 5 pairs, the AI text's `confidence` score is at least 0.4 higher than the human text's. This must hold in all three trials.

**Why this target:** If the two kinds of text don't score far apart, the labels are just noise. 0.4 is wide enough to separate the "human" band (below 0.35) from the "AI" band (above 0.65) in a typical pair, and 4 of 5 allows for one odd pair without letting the system off easily.

---

## 3. Label coverage: all three labels are reachable

Using inputs I submit to `POST /submit`, within 20 submissions each of the three labels (high-confidence human, unsure, high-confidence AI) appears at least once, 3 of 3. This must hold in all three trials.

**Why this target:** A label that no score can produce means my score ranges have a gap, and a reader would never see it. The "unsure" label matters most, because it's what protects a writer when the system isn't sure.

---

## 4. The appeal path: an appeal always changes status and is always recorded

For 5 submitted items, an appeal to `POST /appeal` returns status `under_review`, and `GET /log` shows a matching appeal entry for that `content_id`. 5 of 5. This must hold in all three trials.

**Why this target:** The appeal is the only thing standing between a wrong accusation and nothing at all. If even one appeal fails to change status or leaves no record, a writer's challenge disappears, so I accept no misses.

---

## 5. Bad input: broken requests get a clean 400, never a crash

I send 5 bad inputs to `POST /submit`: missing `text`, empty `text`, whitespace-only `text`, `text` over 10,000 characters, and a non-string `text`. All 5 return status 400 with an error message in the response, and none returns a 500. This must hold in all three trials.

**Why this target:** A crash on malformed input is one of the most common real failures, and a 500 tells a writer nothing about what to fix. Five out of five is realistic because every case is something I can check for before the signals run.
